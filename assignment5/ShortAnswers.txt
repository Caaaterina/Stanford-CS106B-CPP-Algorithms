Q1:

Yes. Even if the contract’s explicit optimization criteria focused on efficiency
and fraud reduction, engineers still have a professional responsibility to
account for foreseeable harm—especially wrongful denials that can directly
threaten people’s health, housing, and food security. “Efficiency” is not
value-neutral: if the system becomes cheaper by increasing false negatives
(denying eligible applicants), that is a serious safety and ethics failure, not
a success.

So the objective function should include constraints and metrics for human
impact (e.g., minimize wrongful denials, ensure due-process safeguards, and keep
appeal/recertification pathways accessible), even if that reduces pure
throughput. At minimum, the design should enforce hard constraints (upper
bounds) on error rates and require human review for high-risk decisions, rather
than optimizing only for cost and fraud.




Q2:

I would start by redefining the goals with all stakeholders (case workers,
applicants, advocates, and the state) and writing them as measurable
requirements: reduce processing time and fraud *while* maintaining access,
minimizing wrongful denials, preserving due process, and keeping
communication/document handling reliable. I’d treat this as a high-stakes,
human-impact system, so “do no harm” constraints come first.

Algorithmically, I’d use analysis tools to meet performance goals without
sacrificing accuracy or fairness: profile bottlenecks (I/O, queueing, document
intake), choose data structures that reduce wait time, and design workflows
that are robust to missing/late documents. For eligibility and fraud detection,
I’d use conservative decision thresholds, calibrate models on representative
data, and optimize for low false-negative rates (wrongful denial) with explicit
cost weights. High-risk or uncertain cases would be routed to human review,
with clear explanations and audit logs.

To avoid the harms described, I’d build safeguards into the process: confirmed
receipt tracking for documents, redundant channels (online/mail/in-person),
accessible callbacks, transparent status updates, and an appeals process that is
easy to use. Finally, I’d continuously monitor outcomes (denial rates, error
rates, demographics, wait times) and run regular audits, updating the system
when metrics indicate real-world harm.
